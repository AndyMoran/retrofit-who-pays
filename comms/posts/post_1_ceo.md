Insulating a rented home pays back. Just not to the person who pays for it.

The landlord buys the insulation. The tenant, who pays the bills, keeps every penny of the saving. So "how many years to payback?" divides one person's cost by another person's saving. Nobody actually experiences that number.

Split it properly and the case changes shape.

The tenant saves £340–1,200 a year from day one, depending on the home, with no outlay, plus warmth.
The landlord pays roughly £2,500–5,400 per home after the Warm Homes grant, and gets none of the bill saving. What the landlord gets back is protection: excess cold becomes a legal hazard on 30 November, damp and mould repairs cost the sector billions, and there's a 2030 compliance date.

That's a risk-and-obligation case, not a payback case. It's stronger than the bill arithmetic suggests, and it doesn't fit in a payback table, which may be why retrofit keeps getting filed under "cost".

(A correction while I'm here: in August I put a 5.4-year payback on this. My model started from a bare loft, and most lofts are already insulated. Beyond the loft, the walls, floor and windows take 9 to 24 years on bills alone. That's exactly why bills are the wrong yardstick for the landlord.)
