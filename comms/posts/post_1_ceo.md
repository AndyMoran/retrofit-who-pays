In August I said insulating a Victorian terrace pays back in 5.4 years. It doesn't, and "payback" was the wrong question anyway.

What went wrong: my model started from a bare loft. Most lofts already have insulation, and topping up the rest is cheap. Over a third of my headline saving was the loft. Insulating the walls, floor and windows beyond that pays back in 9 to 24 years on bills alone.

But whose payback? In a rented home the landlord pays and the tenant saves.

The tenant saves £340–1,200 a year from day one, depending on the home, with no outlay, plus warmth.
The landlord pays roughly £2,500–5,400 per home after the Warm Homes grant, and gets none of the bill saving. What the landlord gets back is protection: excess cold becomes a legal hazard on 30 November, damp and mould repairs cost the sector billions, and there's a 2030 compliance date.

That's a risk-and-obligation case, not a payback case. It's stronger than the bill arithmetic suggests, and it doesn't fit in a payback table, which may be why retrofit keeps getting filed under "cost".
