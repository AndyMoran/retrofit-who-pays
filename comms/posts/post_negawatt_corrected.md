We talk a lot about megawatts. We don't talk nearly enough about negawatts: the energy we never have to generate, transmit or pay for.

I modelled a mixed-tenure terrace estate, calibrated to national housing data, to see what a negawatt is actually worth. For a pre-1919 terrace whose loft is already insulated, insulating the walls, floor and windows to EPC C gives, per home:

• 2,600–4,600 kWh a year displaced
• £450–1,200 a year off bills
• 0.35–0.60 tCO2e a year avoided (roughly 0.9–1.8 t on a marginal grid-carbon basis)
• about 1.1 kW of peak demand removed at the coldest hour

It pays back in 9–24 years on bills and lasts 36. That's positive over its life, but slow, and the payback goes to the tenant, not the landlord who pays.

That's the real problem: delivery. The homes that need it most can't front the capital. The landlords who could don't capture the benefit. And the network, which gains real avoided reinforcement, has no route to buy it.

We've built markets for megawatts. We haven't built one for negawatts.

(I first posted this with bigger numbers: 7,600 kWh, ~£2,000 a year, a 5.4-year payback. Those started from a home with a completely uninsulated loft, and most lofts already have insulation. The corrected figures are smaller, and they make the delivery argument stronger.)
